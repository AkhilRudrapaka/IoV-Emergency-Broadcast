1  Abstract

When a vehicle crashes, the vehicles behind it and the emergency services must learn about it quickly and reliably. In a vehicular ad hoc network (VANET) the obvious way, letting every vehicle rebroadcast the warning once, floods the shared radio channel: copies collide and destroy one another (the broadcast storm). A second risk is a false warning sent by a misbehaving vehicle.

This project builds and evaluates a complete emergency-alert workflow on a SUMO urban traffic simulation. A staged incident vehicle creates one DENM-like alert. The alert is released only after a committee of the 5 nearest reputable neighbours has voted: the source's signature and 3 votes (4 BLS12-381 signatures) are aggregated into one certificate that every receiver and roadside unit (RSU) verifies. Dissemination is trust-aware and cluster-based: DBSCAN clusters and reputation-based cluster-head election decide who relays first, distance slots and a random assessment delay order the relays, and a coverage test suppresses rebroadcasts that would reach nobody new. As a stage of the same method, a CH-to-CH route carries the alert along trusted cluster heads to the nearest RSU. RSUs validate each copy and register the event once; a delivery by the CH-to-CH route stage is queued in a local dispatch outbox.

The method is compared with four forwarding baselines that share the same corroboration and radio model: blind flooding, flooding with a random delay, slotted 1-persistence and slotted 1-persistence with a random delay. Every scheme runs on the identical traffic snapshot of a scenario. Over 30 seeds at 200 vehicles, the proposed method sent 26.4 ± 1.4 frames per alert in its broadcast against 185.8 ± 0.3 for blind flooding (its CH-to-CH route stage is measured as a separate dissemination on the same record: 10.8 ± 1.7 frames), lost 151 ± 35 receptions to collisions against 9632 ± 208, and reached 100.0 ± 0.0 % of the reachable vehicles; it needed 35.3 ± 1.8 ms to reach 90 % of them, against 31.8 ± 1.1 ms for blind flooding. With full protection, a forged alert was accepted by 0 % of honest vehicles, against 100 % without voting. Against the strongest baseline, slotted 1-persistence with a random delay, the differences are mixed (section 15).

Scope. The accident is staged: the project does not implement sensor-based crash detection. "Detection" in this report means the incident vehicle's own report and its corroboration by neighbouring vehicles. The radio is a simulated IEEE 802.11p model, and every result comes from simulation.

2  Problem Statement and Motivation

An emergency alert in a VANET has three requirements at once: it must reach the vehicles around the incident within tens of milliseconds, it must reach the infrastructure (an RSU) so that the emergency services are informed, and it must be trustworthy. All vehicles share one radio channel (IEEE 802.11p, 300 m range in this project).

Why uncontrolled broadcasting is a problem

Under blind flooding every vehicle that receives the alert rebroadcasts it once. Most of these rebroadcasts reach vehicles that already hold the alert, but each occupies the channel; vehicles within range of each other transmit at nearly the same time and their frames collide at the receivers between them (hidden terminals make this worse). The project measures this directly: at 200 vehicles blind flooding put 185.8 ± 0.3 frames on air for one alert and 9632 ± 208 receptions were destroyed by collisions (mean ± 95 % confidence interval over 30 seeds). At 500 vehicles the losses grow to 61705 ± 759.

Why trust matters

An alert that is simply believed can be forged: a misbehaving vehicle could claim an accident that never happened and divert traffic or emergency services. In the project's forged-alert test, an alert without corroboration was accepted by 100 % of the honest vehicles at 200 vehicles, and an RSU registered it in 100 % of the runs.

Problem addressed

Deliver a corroborated emergency alert to the vehicles around an incident and to an RSU with far fewer redundant transmissions and collisions than flooding, without losing coverage, while rejecting forged alerts; and show this fairly, against implemented baselines, on identical traffic.

7  Traffic Simulation

Road network. network/urban.net.xml: a straight 5 × 5 street grid (about 820 × 800 m) with 9 signalised junctions and a 3-lane central boulevard. Two RSUs stand at intersections (RSU-1 at (160, 590) m, RSU-2 at (610, 170) m); their 300 m radio range leaves the far corners uncovered, so alerts from there must be relayed vehicle to vehicle.

Vehicle generation. For a density of N vehicles, N vehicles are inserted at times drawn uniformly from the first 30 s, each on a random-walk route of 30 edges without U-turns (SUMO's default passenger car with Krauss car-following). The densities studied are 50, 100, 200, 250, 300, 500 vehicles; the demonstration uses 200.

Movement. SUMO advances in 1 s steps (--step-length 1 --seed); positions, speeds and lanes are read every second. A watchdog model observes every vehicle with a neighbour once per second and builds its Beta reputation (trust = (s + 1) / (s + f + 2)); 10 % of the vehicles misbehave (they receive but never relay), and an honest relay is missed with probability 0.1.

Scenario and seed. One seed fixes everything random in a run: seed = 10000 + 100·N + r for run r. The scenario ID names it (for example d200_r02 = 200 vehicles, run 2, seed 30002); the same seed always gives the same scenario (validation/test_pipeline.py).

8  Accident / Incident Simulation

Incident selection. For each seed the incident second is drawn from U{60..120} s (after the last departure, so traffic is mixed), and the incident vehicle is drawn at random among the honest vehicles on the road at that second. The location is therefore wherever that vehicle is: always on a lane of the network. For the demonstration pairs, demo_pairs.select takes the scenarios in a fixed seeded order and skips any whose incident lies within 50 m of an incident already used, so consecutive pairs show different places; the choice never looks at any algorithm's result.

Incident information. The incident vehicle creates one alert, identified like an ETSI DENM actionID (station + sequence number). Table 5 shows the alert of scenario d200_r02 as recorded.

Effect on nearby vehicles. The alert reaches the other vehicles only through the radio (section 9–10). In the SUMO-GUI replay, SUMO's clock is paused at the incident second while the tens of milliseconds of radio events are shown in slow motion; afterwards the clock resumes, the crashed vehicle stays stopped and the vehicles behind it react through SUMO's car-following.

How reports are processed. Each incident produces exactly one report (alert). Receivers and RSUs verify its certificate; an RSU registers the event ID once and records every later copy as a duplicate. The RSU code also contains rsu.register_reports, which can merge several valid reports of one crash (same type, within 10 m and 2 s of every report already in an incident). It is covered by unit tests only: the simulation never creates a second report of a crash, so it is not part of the demonstrated workflow (see section 16).

9  Baseline Algorithms

All four baselines use exactly the same corroboration as the proposed method (committee, 3 BLS votes, certificate check by every receiver), the same radio model, the same per-event duplicate state (a vehicle decides once, on its first accepted copy) and the same hop limit; misbehaving vehicles never relay and RSUs only receive. They differ only in the forwarding rule, and none of them uses clustering, cluster heads or the CH-to-CH route. Each was replayed alone in SUMO-GUI in its own comparison pair.

9.1  Blind flooding

Every vehicle that accepts the certified alert rebroadcasts it once, as soon as its certificate has been verified (Flooding.first_copy: the timer is the acceptance slot). Duplicates are counted but never suppress anything, so every honest receiver transmits. Reference: Ni et al. 1999 (broadcast storm).

9.2  Flooding + random delay

As blind flooding, but each rebroadcast waits an extra random assessment delay drawn from U[0, 5 ms) (Flooding(rad=True)). The jitter spreads the transmissions in time; every honest receiver still transmits. Reference: the same jitter the proposed scheme uses (audit RAD-1).

9.3  Slotted 1-persistence

A receiver waits S(D)·τ, where the distance slot S = Ns − ⌈Ns·min(D, R)/R⌉ gives the farthest receivers slot 0 (Ns = 5, τ = 5 ms, R = 300 m). If it hears any duplicate before its slot it suppresses; otherwise it rebroadcasts (Slotted1P). Reference: Wisitpongphan et al. 2007.

10  Proposed Approach

10.1  Clustering and cluster-head election

At the incident second the vehicles are clustered with DBSCAN (ε = 40 m, MinPts = 3). Inside each cluster, cluster heads are elected greedily: repeatedly the trusted vehicle (trust ≥ 0.7) whose radio range covers the most still-uncovered members, ties broken by the lowest relative speed to its neighbours. Isolated vehicles (DBSCAN noise) are bridges. Trusted cluster heads and trusted bridges form tier 0, everyone else tier 1 (clustering.elect_roles).

10.2  Committee formation and voting

The incident vehicle broadcasts a signed PROPOSAL. The committee is the 5 nearest neighbours of the incident vehicle whose trust is at least 0.7. Honest members, who observe the event, each answer with one VOTE (a BLS signature over the event digest) in rank order, 0.5 ms apart; misbehaving members stay silent. The source counts distinct voters and releases the alert at 3 votes (corroboration.plan, Protocol.on_receive).

10.3  Certificate and signature verification

The source aggregates its own signature and the votes into one BLS12-381 signature (96 bytes, real cryptography with py_ecc) and broadcasts the certified alert. Every receiver and RSU applies the same rule (corroboration.accept): the first signer must be the source, the aggregate signature must verify, and at least 3 endorsers must be reputable. Processing time is charged as 1.4 ms per signature and 4.3 ms per verification.

10.4  Forwarding control

A vehicle decides once, on its first accepted copy. It waits (tier·Ns + S(D))·τ + U[0, RAD): tier-0 relays decide a full slot cycle before tier 1, farther receivers before nearer ones, and the random assessment delay separates vehicles in the same slot. When the wait ends it applies a coverage test: it rebroadcasts only if at least one beacon neighbour (vehicle or RSU) is not within range of any sender it has heard; otherwise it suppresses (protocols.Proposed). An uncovered RSU counts like any neighbour, which is what carries the alert to the infrastructure.

10.5  Duplicate suppression

Every vehicle keeps per-event state: a copy of an alert it already holds is never processed again, but its sender is added to the set of senders heard, which is exactly what the coverage test uses. Suppression is therefore driven by duplicates without any vehicle ever rebroadcasting twice. RSUs register an event ID once and record every later copy as DUPLICATE.

10.6  CH-to-CH routing (a stage of the proposed method)

The route is computed with Dijkstra (by distance) from the incident vehicle to the nearest RSU over cluster heads with trust ≥ 0.7 (infrastructure.shortest_route). Each head on the route first collects a local quorum (itself plus cluster members' votes) and then forwards; vehicles off the route stay silent (protocols.CHRoute). There is no fallback: without a route, or if a head's local quorum fails, nothing is delivered on this path. It is part of the proposed method — the path that writes the dispatch outbox — not a competing algorithm. In scenario d200_r02 the route was v164 → v32 → RSU-2.

10.7  RSU verification and registration

An RSU decodes a copy, verifies its certificate (4.3 ms) and runs plausibility checks on its own clock: required fields, a known message type, a position inside the road network, not created after reception, at most 600 s old, within the hop limit (10), and the certificate. The first valid copy is REGISTERED (registration time = arrival + verification), later copies are DUPLICATE and failures REJECTED with their reason (rsu.register). The CH-route delivery is written to a local SQLite dispatch outbox (infrastructure.persist); no real emergency service is contacted.

10.8  Forged-alert rejection

On the same traffic, a misbehaving vehicle claims an accident that did not happen and its misbehaving neighbours endorse it. Their signatures are valid, but they are not reputable, so the certificate fails the acceptance rule at every receiver and RSU.

11  Dashboard and Demonstration

The faculty demonstration is organised around comparison pairs. A pair is one incident scenario replayed twice in SUMO-GUI: once with a single baseline, once with the proposed method (its CH-to-CH route stage and the forged-alert test included). The two runs never share a map and never run at the same time. Both runs re-simulate the pair's seed, so they replay the same record; the dashboard shows the comparison only when both fingerprints are equal.

Controls

  • New pair takes the next scenario of the fixed seeded order (incident on the network, ≥ 50 m from every incident used before) with the baseline chosen in the list beside it.
  • Run baseline only / Run proposed only start one run in its own process; SUMO-GUI opens and plays the run from start to finish (in this report's pairs a baseline-only run took 42–55 s and a proposed-only run 96–121 s). While a run is active every start button is disabled and the server refuses another start (HTTP 409). Pause/resume is not offered.
  • A run whose process ends without finishing is marked failed; its live state and captures are never shown as current. If a SUMO-GUI window stops responding (seen once in about 15 runs), closing it ends the run this way, and the run can be started again on the same record; a window capture never waits more than 10 s for SUMO-GUI.

15  Results and Discussion

The statements below are generated from the results and checked when this report is built; each names its source.

  • At 200 vehicles the proposed method's broadcast sent 26.4 ± 1.4 frames per alert, 7.0 times fewer than blind flooding (185.8 ± 0.3; its CH-to-CH route stage is a separate dissemination, counted in its own row); paired over the seeds the difference is -159.3 ± 1.4 frames. (summary.csv, paired.csv)
  • Against the strongest baseline, slotted 1-p + random delay (24.1 ± 1.0 frames), the proposed method shows more frames than it at 200 vehicles (paired difference +2.3 ± 1.6). (summary.csv, paired.csv)
  • Receptions lost to collisions: 151 ± 35 for the proposed method against 9632 ± 208 for blind flooding and 188 ± 55 for slotted 1-p + random delay (no significant difference than the latter: -36 ± 55). (summary.csv, paired.csv)
  • The proposed method reached 100 % of the reachable vehicles in every run at every density. Blind flooding reached 99.9 ± 0.1 % and slotted 1-persistence 99.6 ± 0.5 % at 200 vehicles. (summary.csv)
  • Delay is the trade-off: the proposed method needed 35.3 ± 1.8 ms to reach 90 % of the reachable vehicles against 31.8 ± 1.1 ms for blind flooding (+3.5 ± 1.7 ms paired; significantly slower). (summary.csv, paired.csv)
  • First RSU registration took 21.4 ± 1.9 ms with the proposed method and 22.0 ± 1.9 ms with blind flooding (paired -0.6 ± 1.0 ms: no significant difference); every scheme delivered to an RSU in 100 ± 0 % of the runs. (summary.csv, paired.csv)
  • The CH-to-CH route stage used 10.8 ± 1.7 frames and lost no receptions, but by design it informs only the vehicles along and around the route (35.9 ± 4.3 % reach) and delivered to the RSU in 93 ± 9 % of the runs (it has no fallback). (summary.csv)
  • With full protection no honest vehicle accepted a forged alert and no RSU registered one, at any density; without the reputation gate 13 %, 53 %, 87 %, 97 %, 97 %, 100 % of honest vehicles accepted it (50, 100, 200, 250, 300, 500 vehicles). (forgery_summary.csv)
  • Against the strongest baseline, slotted 1-p + random delay, paired over the seeds at every density, the proposed method is mixed: frames on air worse at 100, 200 vehicles; receptions lost better at 250, 300, 500 vehicles; worse at 100 vehicles; reach better at 50, 100, 500 vehicles; time to 90 % better at 50 vehicles; worse at 300, 500 vehicles; first RSU registration worse at 50, 200, 300 vehicles (a density not named shows no significant difference). (paired.csv)
  • In the comparison pairs, the proposed method's broadcast used fewer frames than the replayed baseline in 5 of 5 pairs (its CH-to-CH route stage is counted separately in the pair tables; single runs illustrate, the seeds above decide). (pair records)

Discussion

What the experiments demonstrate. Controlling who rebroadcasts and when removes most of flooding's redundant transmissions and, with them, most collisions, without giving up coverage: at 200 vehicles the proposed method sent 26.4 ± 1.4 frames per alert against 185.8 ± 0.3 for blind flooding, and it reached every reachable vehicle in every run at every density. The cost is time: waiting for tiers, slots and the coverage test makes it slower than flooding to reach 90 % of the vehicles.

Against the strongest baseline. Slotted 1-persistence with a random delay already removes most of flooding's redundancy. Paired over the 30 seeds of each density (fewer for time to 90 % where a run never reached it), the proposed method is frames on air worse at 100, 200 vehicles; receptions lost better at 250, 300, 500 vehicles; worse at 100 vehicles; reach better at 50, 100, 500 vehicles; time to 90 % better at 50 vehicles; worse at 300, 500 vehicles; first RSU registration worse at 50, 200, 300 vehicles (a density not named shows no significant difference). The evidence therefore supports the proposed method as a corroborated, trust-aware scheme of efficiency comparable to the best baseline, not as uniformly superior to it. Its distinguishing contributions are the trust-aware choice of relays, the coverage test and the CH-to-CH route that delivers to the RSU and writes the dispatch. The corroboration that rejects forged alerts belongs to the whole system: every baseline was given the same corroboration, so that the comparison isolates forwarding.

Corroboration. Voting with a reputation gate rejected every forged alert in the forged-alert test, while voting alone was defeated by colluding misbehaving neighbours whenever enough of them were in range; this result depends on the reputation being reliable (section 16).

What is not shown. A single comparison pair is one run of one scenario: it illustrates the behaviour and lets the replays be compared like for like, but supports no statistical claim. General statements rest on the 30-seed experiment and its paired confidence intervals.

16  Limitations

  • Simulated radio. IEEE 802.11p on a unit disk (300 m), simulated slot by slot with carrier sensing, collisions, hidden terminals and half-duplex losses; fading, capture, background beacon load and real hardware are not modelled.
  • Staged incident, no crash detection. The accident is designated, not detected from sensors; honest committee members are assumed to observe it.
  • One report per incident. The simulation creates exactly one alert per crash. rsu.register_reports, which can merge several valid reports of one crash (same type, within 10 m and 2 s of every report of an incident), is unit-tested but not part of the simulated workflow; integrating it would need a multi-reporter scenario model. Its rule cannot tell two distinct crashes within those bounds apart.
  • Near-oracle reputation. Misbehaving vehicles are persistent black holes, so their trust stays low by construction. On-off attackers that behave well while watched can pass the trust gate, and forged alerts are then accepted (auditor measurement: registered in 8 of 10 runs; docs/AUDIT.md).
  • Keys from vehicle IDs; fixed BLS timing. There is no public-key infrastructure; signing and verification are charged fixed times.
  • Fail-closed corroboration. A genuine alert is never released when fewer than 3 reputable honest voters are in range; a quorum-timeout fallback is specified but not implemented.
  • CH-to-CH route has no fallback. Without a route, or if a head's local quorum fails, nothing is delivered on that path.
  • Frozen snapshot and small map. Dissemination is computed on the incident-second snapshot (it lasts tens of ms); the network is one 5 × 5 grid and the parameters are untuned design choices.
  • Demonstration. SUMO-GUI replays a run computed by the experiment engine; window captures need Windows; runs cannot be paused; the dashboard is a local tool without authentication; the pair scenarios are the 30 seeds at 200 vehicles, and a location is reused (and labelled) only after all others.
  • Statistics. Pairs are single runs; multi-seed results use 30 seeds per density with Student-t 95 % intervals (metrics.summarize), also for the paired differences.

18  Conclusion and Future Scope

The project implements the complete workflow from SUMO traffic to a registered, corroborated emergency alert: reproducible scenarios, alert creation, committee voting with real BLS aggregate signatures, trust-aware clustering and controlled forwarding, a CH-to-CH route to the RSU, RSU validation and single registration, metrics, and a demonstration in which the baseline and the proposed method are replayed separately and compared on the identical record. Against four implemented baselines on identical traffic, the proposed method cut frames on air and collision losses by large factors relative to flooding, kept reach at 100 % in the 30-seed experiment at 200 vehicles, and rejected every forged alert with full protection, at the price of a few milliseconds of extra delay to reach 90 % of the vehicles. Against the strongest baseline, slotted 1-persistence with a random delay, the results are mixed (section 15).

Future scope

  • A richer channel model (fading, capture, beacon load) and validation against a network simulator or hardware.
  • Sensor-based crash detection and multi-reporter incidents, which would put rsu.register_reports into the workflow.
  • A reputation that resists on-off attackers, a real PKI, and a quorum-timeout fallback.
  • A fallback for the CH-to-CH route, tuning of the slot, delay and committee parameters, and larger, real road networks.
  • Pausable live (not replayed) visualisation, and authentication for a multi-user dashboard.
